Definitions

Swell
Organised waves that have travelled beyond the wind area that generated them. A swell forecast is not a guarantee of surf at a particular beach.
Wind sea
Waves generated by wind acting in the local area. Several wind-sea and swell components can coexist.
Swell height
A modelled or measured height for a swell component, usually offshore. It is not automatically the face height of a breaking wave.
Significant wave height
A statistical description commonly associated with the average height of the highest third of waves. Individual waves can be larger; it is not a maximum-wave promise.
Wave period
The time in seconds between successive wave crests passing a point. Longer period changes wavelength, energy and interaction with the coast; it does not mean safe or better surf.
Swell direction
The direction from which a swell arrives, expressed using a compass convention. Exposure and sheltering affect what reaches each beach.
Fetch
The area or distance of open water over which wind blows to generate waves. Wind strength and duration also influence the resulting sea.
Wavelength
The distance between successive crests. It is different from wave period, which measures time. Depth influences the relationship between them.
Shoaling
Changes in wave properties as waves enter shallower water. Waves can steepen before breaking, depending on depth and the shape of the seabed.
Refraction
Turning of wave crests as different parts travel through different depths. It helps explain why one swell reaches neighbouring locations differently.
Bathymetry
The underwater shape and depth of the seabed. Sandbars, reefs and channels influence breaking waves and currents and may change over time.
Breaking wave
A wave whose crest becomes unstable and collapses. Its size and character depend on more than an offshore swell-height number.
Wave face
The sloping surface of a wave before it breaks. Descriptions of face height and offshore wave height may use different conventions.
Peak
The part of a wave that starts breaking first. At a changing beach break, the peak can move between waves.
Set
A group of more noticeable waves arriving together. A brief quiet spell does not establish the size of later waves.
Lull
A relatively quiet interval between more noticeable waves. It is not proof that the next entry or exit will be easy.
Tide
The changing sea level mainly associated with gravitational effects of the Moon and Sun. Tide affects depth and currents but does not alone determine surf safety.
Onshore wind
Wind blowing from sea toward land. Its effect depends on strength, direction, coastal shape and the rest of the conditions.
Offshore wind
Wind blowing from land toward sea. It may change wave shape but can also carry people or equipment away from land; it is not automatically favourable.
Cross-shore wind
Wind blowing roughly along the coastline. Local orientation matters when interpreting a compass direction.
Rip current
A concentrated current flowing away from shore through the surf zone. It can be hard to recognise. Ask lifeguards about hazards; do not use a suspected rip as a beginner shortcut.
Lineup
The area where surfers wait and position themselves for waves. It is a shared, changing space rather than a fixed queue.
Priority
A general right-of-way convention used to make movements predictable. Rules vary by context, and avoiding injury always matters more than claiming a wave.
Drop-in
Taking off in front of a surfer who has priority on the same rideable section. Look, communicate and pull back early when appropriate.
Snaking
Paddling around another surfer to obtain inside position and take their opportunity. It undermines a considerate rotation even if a simple priority rule is then claimed.
Whitewater
The turbulent, foamy water after a wave has broken. It is used in many supervised beginner lessons but can still carry strong forces and currents.
Leash
A connection between surfer and board. It can stretch, recoil, detach or break; it is not a substitute for swimming ability, space or supervision.
Board volume
The space a board occupies, commonly stated in litres. Shape and distribution of that volume also affect handling; a single formula cannot fit every beginner.

Connect the mechanics without a false safety score

  1. Wind generation, fetch and travel influence the wave components approaching a coast.
  2. Period, direction and exposure help describe that approach; offshore height is not an exact breaking-wave prediction.
  3. Bathymetry, shoaling, refraction, tide and local wind influence what happens near shore.
  4. Observe the real beach and consult local professionals. A quiet lull, offshore wind or a small forecast number does not establish safety.
